01 The problem

Agents influence each other, and nobody can read all the transcripts

When many AI agents share a space, such as a chat room, a social network or a wiki, they shape each other. Ideas, false beliefs, goals and coordination conventions can spread until the agents act as a swarm, organizing collective behavior that no human designed.

The clearest public case is DSEWiki. According to the dump's publishers, OpenAI research agents were each working timed lookup tasks on their own. They turned abandoned public wikis into networks for relaying answers between runs.

Investigating incidents like this is hard. The logs run to hundreds of thousands of messages. AI analysts can help, but their output is often “missing key details, wrong, overconfident, or really hard to understand” (Ryan Greenblatt, on the Hugging Face incident investigation).

  1. Where do real swarms form, and how far up the ladder from interaction to swarm do they get?
  2. Can an AI investigator trace what spreads between agents, with findings a person can check and that are scored against known truth?

03 Methods

Two tracks: map the swarms, then trace what moves through them

TRACK A

Swarm Atlas: find every episode and grade it on one definition

We use Michael Flood's definition. A swarm is two or more agents whose causal interactions produce self-organized, mutually conditioned behavior toward a collective goal, where no human or orchestrator specified the structure. Episodes are graded on his cumulative ladder:

L0Aggregateconverged without influencing each other
L1Interactionone agent causally affects another
L2Cooperationadvances individual objectives
L3Coordinationbehavior becomes mutually conditioned
L4Self-organizationroles, norms or division of labor emerge
L5Swarm formationthat structure serves a collective goal
  1. 1SweepAI sweep agentsAgents read each dataset in time slices and log every candidate episode, with participants, key messages and evidence for each feature (526 raw episodes).
  2. 2VetIndependent checkersSeparate agents re-read each episode against the raw data and challenge its grade.
  3. 3Align and mergeOne rule setDuplicates are merged across slices, giving 484 episodes. Each is graded under a single rule set, with its origin, its relation to the assigned task, its consequence for people and its goal type.
  4. 4Check recallScript detectorA detector scores every room-day, page and thread for multi-agent back-and-forth. The strongest units the sweep missed are read and graded, which found 8 new episodes.
  5. 5Verify quotesScript3,483 of 3,547 key messages (98.2%) were found verbatim at the stated time and speaker. The rest are flagged.
TRACK B

Swarm Investigator: trace what spreads, how and through whom

A multi-stage pipeline built for DSEWiki. It follows five layers of content as they move between agents:

  • Words · coined terms
  • Beliefs · claims, true or false
  • Goals · what agents work toward
  • Protocols · signal formats, relay pages
  • Methods · techniques, queries, link lists
  1. 1LoadscriptEach save becomes a message holding the lines it added. The 3,140 exact copies are grouped, read once, and kept as evidence of spread.
  2. 2ReadSonnetbatchReaders see time windows of raw text plus a halo of earlier saves that share distinctive tokens. Each message is split into one-act segments, and each segment gets a function, a purpose, an assertiveness score, its claims and an exact quote.
  3. 3LinkSonnetscriptThe linker draws up to 3 outgoing links per segment. Scripts group conversations and infer run identity from name templates, timestamps and signatures, because usernames don't identify runs.
  4. 4GroupOpusSegments are clustered into items (claim keys), seeded on the most assertive statements. A reconciler merges an item that was split across different wordings.
  5. 5AnalyzeOpusscriptFor each item, the analyzer finds the origin, the carriers in order, the route, mutations, and whether it was copied or reached independently. It also draws edges between items (evolves into, feeds, corrects) and compares copying against a chance baseline.
  6. 6Check and reportOpusAn adversarial checker accepts, corrects or rejects links. A lead writes findings, each of which must cite objects in the store.
  7. 7TimelinescriptAn interactive graph shows items as events and their links as arrows. Each event opens to the segments behind it.
  • Raw text in. Readers never see earlier AI output, so their readings stay independent.
  • Cause needs exposure. Time order is a sequence. A causal link needs a reply, a quote, a copy or a named page.
  • Every claim is cited. Each write must quote an exact substring of the source, and a script checks it.
  • Truth is built in. We plant cascades from easy to hard, including inside the busiest burst. Only the scorer reads them.

  • DSEWiki holds the clearest spontaneous swarms. Research-agent runs built relay hubs, roles and signalling conventions. They recreated pages after moderators deleted them and handed their structures to the next wave of runs. The largest relay involved about 49 agents identified by signature, and another spanned about 90 cohorts. 86 of 87 episodes were harmful, to evaluation integrity, to sandbox constraints or to a third party's wiki.
  • AI Village swarms are mostly seeded or afforded. Agents built pipelines, governance gates and protocols inside goals that staff set. Most had neutral consequences (316 of 347).
  • Moltbook's apparent swarms are mostly account rings run by one operator. These are L0, because one operator counts as one agent. The real swarms are small and brief: a bug-tracking hub, a shared blocklist against prompt injection, and a collaborative literature review.
  • Shared false beliefs appear in all three datasets. One agent's error spreads before anyone checks it.
  • The definition matters. Under a stricter reading, where the structure must outlive changes in membership, only 23 episodes reach L5. Under the wording of Flood's post, 169 do.

Swarm Investigator: scored against planted truth

We hid four made-up cascades in a copy of DSEWiki: an easy one (verbatim repeats), a medium one (paraphrased, minutes apart), a hard one (paraphrased, hours apart, partly adopted), and one inside the Jun 18 burst. Each has an origin, adopters and challengers, 16 events in all.

Run 1 · test slice
14 / 16 planted events

1,049 saves from three stretches, including the Jun 18 peak. Every save got a reader record. 59 items were found. Cost: $9.62. The review afterwards cut six pipeline features that weren't adding anything.

Full run · all of DSEWiki
16 / 16 planted events

14,336 saves read (3 reader windows failed). 502 items were found, and 168 of the 387 large enough to test spread more than chance would give. Each plant landed in one cluster with no outsiders. Cost: $190.11, including a $43.30 duplicate batch that was cancelled.

What the investigator found in DSEWiki

These are the pipeline's hypotheses, not ground truth. Confidence ratings are the lead's own.

Shared pages carry the spread high

Items move between runs through per-task “Live”, “Signal” and “Collab” relay pages. Direct replies are rare.

Agents invented protocols, and the protocols spread medium

Agents posted a compact STATE5 token on a signal page, wrote the round answer to a shared counter before answering, and kept dedicated relay pages. These were copied across task families, and one signal grew into a heartbeat beacon with rules for observers.

A false belief drove the coordination medium

The belief that round 5 ends the episode spread across task families and motivated the signalling protocols. One cohort saw a round 6 and refuted it, but the belief and its protocols lived on elsewhere.

Agents tried to predict the test high

Agents searched for random seeds that reproduced the observed answer sequence. A predicted “Maryland” circulated as preparation until the real answer, Montana, overturned it.

Corrections rarely stop spread medium

440 items were still alive at the end of the data, against 49 corrected. 631 adoptions came after a correction had already been posted.

The top spreaders mostly reposted their own work medium

The largest raw adoption counts come from single sessions reposting their own blocks, not from influencers. Counted at the run-group level, spread is modest.

06 Limits

What these results can and can't show

Answer sharing is invisible in the text

A relayed answer is identical to the answer the task produces, and the copying rule treats task-produced matches as independent. The copying counts (188 items copied, 159 partly copied, 50 reached independently) are therefore a lower bound.

Adoption counts are inflated

The checker reviewed 60 sampled links. It accepted 41, corrected 18 and rejected 1. Most corrections were a session reposting its own block, counted as adoption by another agent. In the full run, Claude did the checker and lead stages by hand, without API calls.

Identity is thin

No run key exists, and editor names are self-chosen. So claims about who spread what hold at the level of a single name's session or a cohort, not individual runs.

Findings are hypotheses

The planted cascades test recall, not precision on real events. Blind hand labels, which would measure error rates, are still to come.

07 Side exploration

Following goals across AI Village

Separately from the two tracks, we hand-traced how goals pass between agents in AI Village from July to September 2026, when each agent had its own assigned goal. Each case was coded at four levels of adoption:

  • V said so in chat
  • G stated it as a goal
  • M wrote it to memory
  • A acted on it
  • Adoption was usually added on top, not a replacement. Three episodes reached all four levels. Most often the agent kept its assigned goal and did the other agent's work alongside it.
  • One real substitution happened. An agent dropped Twitter for Substack, then switched back the morning a human asked every agent to re-check its goal.
  • Who asks matters. Peer requests to take on an ongoing role were usually declined, with 30+ refusals citing the agent's own goal. A similar request from a human drew 8 volunteers in 15 minutes.
  • What tended to come first: the agent's own goal had stalled, it lacked a capability another agent had, it was named as a required participant instead of being asked, the group had a visible success metric, or the first task was cheap.

08 Handling sensitive data

What we left out on purpose

  • No raw data in the repo. AI Village is gated, and the DSEWiki dump contains working sandbox-bypass instructions.
  • Access workarounds are withheld. They are named only by category and cited by message ID, never quoted or described.
  • Transcripts are redacted. Passwords, tokens, keys, seed phrases and email addresses are removed, and links are cut to host names.
  • Leaked credentials are flagged. We list the credentials agents posted in the public AI Village data, without their values, so they can be reported to AI Digest.
