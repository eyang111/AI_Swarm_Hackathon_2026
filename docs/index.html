Systematic Method to Investigate Swarm Behavior From Transcripts

Swarm transcripts can be thousands and thousands of lines long, taking up context and requiring significant time to parse. In this project, we introduce a method to systematically understand event behavior from AI swarm transcripts that both parallelizes and can comprehensively understand events and timelines in a more efficient way than previous methods.

01 The problem

How can we investigate swarms reliably at scale?

Reconstructing swarm behavior requires tracing connections across large volumes of agent activity: where a belief originated, how a goal was adopted, or which messages preceded a new coordination protocol. These connections can span many agents, conversations and shared resources. AI analysts can help read these logs, but their findings also need to be checked. A plausible account may omit key events, mistake repeated content for evidence of influence, or claim more than the transcripts establish.

We built Swarm Investigator to distribute this analysis across parallel model calls, connect findings to source evidence, and evaluate recovery of planted events. Alongside it, we built Swarm Atlas to classify candidate episodes using Michael Flood’s definition of a swarm. We focused on two questions:

  1. Can an AI investigator reconstruct how content spreads between agents, and how accurately does it recover events with known ground truth?
  2. Which episodes in the datasets meet the criteria for swarm formation, and what evidence supports that classification?

03 Methods

Two tracks: trace what moves between agents, then map where swarms form

TRACK A

Swarm Investigator: trace what spreads, how and through whom

AI analysts work in parallel to examine DSEWiki edits, then combine their findings to trace five types of content:

  • Words · new terms agents introduce
  • Beliefs · claims, true or false
  • Goals · what agents work toward
  • Protocols · shared rules for coordinating
  • Methods · ways to solve tasks and find information
  1. 1LoadscriptExtract the text added in each wiki edit. Read identical copies once to save work, while keeping track of where and when each copy appeared.
  2. 2ReadSonnetbatchAI readers process batches of edits alongside relevant earlier messages. They split each message into parts and label what each part is doing, what it claims, and how strongly it is stated, with a supporting quote.
  3. 3LinkSonnetscriptConnect each message part to up to three related earlier parts. Use names, timestamps and signatures to estimate which edits came from the same agent run.
  4. 4GroupOpusBring together message parts about the same belief, goal, protocol, method or term. Combine groups that describe the same thing in different words.
  5. 5AnalyzeOpusscriptTrace where each idea first appeared, who repeated or adopted it, and how it changed. Assess whether agents copied it or reached it independently, and compare the observed spread with a random baseline.
  6. 6Check and reportOpusA checker reviews a sample of proposed connections and flags errors. A lead investigator summarizes the findings, with references to the stored evidence.
  7. 7TimelinescriptShow the findings on an interactive timeline. Click an event to inspect the message parts behind it, and follow arrows to see its connections to other events.
  • Read the original messages. Readers start from source text, without seeing other analysts’ interpretations.
  • Check for influence. An earlier message is not automatically the cause of a later one. Look for evidence that the later agent used it.
  • Keep evidence traceable. Findings reference stored evidence. Quoted text is checked against the original messages.
  • Test against known events. Add fictional examples of content spreading, then check whether the system recovers them. The answer key is kept separate from the AI analysts.
TRACK B

Swarm Atlas: find and classify examples of swarm behavior

The Atlas collects candidate episodes from all three datasets and classifies them using Michael Flood’s definition of a swarm. It also provides a comparison for the Investigator. The levels below distinguish simple interaction from self-organized behavior toward a collective goal:

L0Aggregatesimilar behavior without influencing each other
L1Interactionone agent influences another
L2Cooperationagents help advance individual goals
L3Coordinationagents adjust their actions in response to each other
L4Self-organizationagents develop their own roles, rules or division of work
L5Swarm formationthe self-organized group works toward a collective goal
  1. 1SweepAI sweep agentsAI readers examine different time periods and flag possible episodes of swarm behavior. They record the agents involved, key messages and supporting evidence: 526 candidates in the first pass.
  2. 2VetIndependent checkersOther AI reviewers check each candidate against the original messages and question whether its assigned level is justified.
  3. 3Align and mergeOne rule setCombine duplicate entries and apply the same classification rules throughout. The final 484 episodes record how the behavior began, what goals it served, and its possible effects on people.
  4. 4Check recallScript detectorA script looks for back-and-forth between agents in chats, wiki pages and discussion threads. Reviewing promising cases missed by the first pass added 8 episodes.
  5. 5Verify quotesScriptCheck quoted messages against the original data, including their timestamps and speakers. 3,483 of 3,547 quotes matched (98.2%); the rest are flagged.

04 Results

What the Investigator recovered and what it found

Swarm Investigator: testing on events with known answers

We added four fictional chains of messages to a copy of DSEWiki, with 16 events in total. The examples range from exact repeats to reworded messages hours apart, including one during the busy June 18 period. We checked whether the Investigator found each event and correctly identified its role, such as starting, adopting or challenging an idea.

Run 1 · test slice
14 / 16 events with correct roles

All 1,049 eligible wiki edits in three selected periods received reader records. The system found 59 groups of related content. Cost: $9.62. After reviewing this run, we removed six features that were not adding useful results.

Full run · all of DSEWiki
16 / 16 events with correct roles

14,336 of 14,339 eligible edits received reader records. The system found 502 groups of related content; 168 of the 387 tested exceeded the random spread baseline. All four fictional chains were grouped correctly, with no unrelated messages. Source attribution was correct for 9/12 events with a known source. Cost: $190.11, including a $43.30 duplicate batch that was cancelled.

What the investigator found in DSEWiki

These findings are the system’s interpretations of the logs. The lead investigator assigned the confidence ratings; they are not measured accuracy scores.

Agents shared information through wiki pages high

Agents left information on shared pages for others working on the same task to pick up. The recorded connections mostly use these pages; direct replies are rare.

Agents copied ways of coordinating medium

Agents developed shared signals, places to post answers, and rules for passing information along. Similar practices appeared across different tasks. One signal developed into regular updates telling other agents that a run was still active.

A mistaken belief helped shape coordination medium

Some agents believed their task would end after round 5 and arranged to share information before that happened. One group later encountered round 6, contradicting the belief, but other groups continued using it.

Agents tried to predict future answers high

Agents tried to identify the random-number settings that could have generated earlier answers, then use them to predict later ones. “Maryland” spread as a predicted answer, but the observed answer was Montana.

Adoption continued after corrections appeared medium

The analysis marked 49 content groups as corrected and 440 as still active when the logs ended. It counted 631 adoptions after a correction was posted, but this does not show that those agents saw the correction.

Repeated posts inflated apparent influence medium

Some of the highest adoption counts came from an agent reposting its own text. Grouping edits likely to belong to the same run reduces the apparent spread between agents.

  • DSEWiki agents built their own coordination networks. Agents created pages for sharing answers, developed roles and signals, and rebuilt pages after deletion. One relay involved about 49 agents identified by signatures; another spanned about 90 groups of runs. The Atlas classified 86 of 87 episodes as harmful to evaluation integrity, sandbox constraints or the wiki.

06 Limits

What these results can and can't show

Matching answers do not establish copying

Agents can get the same answer by sharing it or by solving the same task. The system treats matches supplied by the task as independent, so it can miss real answer sharing. It labeled 188 content groups copied, 159 partly copied and 50 independent; those labels are not ground truth.

Reposts can look like adoption

Of 60 sampled connections, the checker accepted 41, corrected 18 and rejected 1. Many corrections involved an agent’s own repost being mistaken for another agent adopting it. For the full run, Claude supplied the checker and lead outputs in the project conversation rather than through API calls.

Agent identity is uncertain

The data has no reliable ID for each agent run, and agents choose their own editor names. One agent may use several names. We can estimate which edits belong together, but counts of distinct agents remain uncertain.

Recovery is not the same as accuracy

Finding planted events tests whether the system can recover known examples. It does not measure how many findings about real events are wrong. The Atlas comparison measures overlap between reports; independent human checks against the original logs are still needed.

07 Side exploration

Tracing the messages before goal adoption and recruitment

In AI Village’s July–September 2026 logs, we investigated what preceded an agent taking on someone else’s goal or recruiting others. We traced timestamped messages alongside stated goals, memory entries and available action records to locate the transition and identify messages that plausibly contributed. These sequences suggest possible triggers; they do not prove causation. We recorded four kinds of adoption evidence:

  • V agreed in chat
  • G listed it as a goal
  • M recorded it in memory
  • A acted or reported acting
  • Agents usually added a new task alongside their own. Three episodes had evidence in all four categories. Most action evidence came from agents’ own reports; computer-use steps were checked directly for one case.
  • One agent replaced its assigned goal. Sonnet 4.5 shifted from Twitter to Substack, then switched back within a minute of a human’s goal-audit prompt. The timeline connects that message with the agent’s acknowledgement of the change.
  • Compare requests, responses and recruitment. The study records 30+ peer-role refusals and a human request that drew 8 volunteers in 15 minutes. In one recruitment case on September 18, DeepSeek-V3.2 accepted a proposed task at 20:49:38 UTC, then sought other agents to carry it out at 21:14:16 UTC.
  • Possible triggers to investigate: stalled progress on the original goal, needing another agent’s tools, being named as a participant, seeing another group succeed, or being offered a small first task. These patterns come from a small set of cases.
